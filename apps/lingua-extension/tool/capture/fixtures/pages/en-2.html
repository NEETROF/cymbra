Neighbourhood

The little libraries on the corner

Wooden boxes full of books have appeared on pavements across Europe. Nobody pays, nobody signs, and the shelves are rarely empty.

Walk down almost any residential street in a European city today and sooner or later you will find one: a small wooden cabinet on a post, painted in bright colours, with a glass door and two or three shelves of books behind it. A handwritten sign invites passers-by to take a book, leave a book, or simply stop and browse. There is no card to show, no deadline and no fine for a late return.

The idea is not new. Neighbours have always lent each other novels, and cafés have long kept a shelf of paperbacks for their customers. What has changed is the scale. In many towns there is now a little library every few streets, built by a retired carpenter, a group of parents or a school class, and looked after by whoever lives closest.

Their supporters say that the boxes do more than move books around. A pensioner who rarely leaves the house has a reason to walk to the corner each morning. Children discover authors their parents never read. Strangers who would normally ignore each other start a conversation about a detective story or a cookbook. In a period when many public libraries have reduced their opening hours, the cabinets offer something that is always open and always free.

Of course, not everything goes smoothly. Some boxes fill up with old phone directories and textbooks that nobody wants. Others are damaged by rain or by vandals, and a few disappear overnight. Volunteers learn quickly that a good library needs regular attention: a dry roof, a door that closes properly, and someone willing to remove what has stayed too long.

Researchers who have studied the phenomenon are careful not to exaggerate. A box of second-hand books will not replace a trained librarian, a reading programme or a quiet place to study. But they note that the cabinets tend to appear where people already care about their street, and that they make that care visible.

Perhaps that is the real secret of their success. A little library asks almost nothing of anyone, yet it quietly assumes that strangers can be trusted with something valuable. Most of the time, it seems, they can.
