The quiet return of the night train

Sleeper carriages almost vanished from Europe. Now they are back, slowly, one route at a time.

For most of the twentieth century, falling asleep in one city and waking up in another was an ordinary part of travelling in Europe. Sleeper carriages crossed borders every evening, carrying students, families and business travellers who preferred a narrow bunk to an early flight. Then, slowly, the trains disappeared. Cheap airlines offered tickets for the price of a dinner, motorways grew longer, and railway companies decided that night services cost too much to maintain. By the early 2010s, many routes that had run for decades were quietly abandoned.

Today the picture is changing again. Several operators have brought back lines that seemed lost for good, and new companies are trying their luck on routes that never existed before. Behind this revival lies a simple idea: if you travel while you sleep, you do not lose a day of your life on the journey. You board the train after work, read for an hour, and arrive in the centre of another capital before breakfast, without queues, security checks or a bus ride from a distant airport.

Climate concerns have also played their part. A seat on an overnight train produces only a fraction of the emissions of the same trip by plane, and a growing number of travellers say they would happily accept a longer journey if it meant a smaller footprint. Governments have noticed. Some have offered subsidies, others have promised to remove the taxes that made rail tickets more expensive than flights.

The comeback has not been smooth, however. Modern sleeper carriages are expensive to build, and manufacturers can take years to deliver them. Tracks are shared with freight trains that also run at night, so timetables must be negotiated country by country. Passengers sometimes complain about delays, broken air conditioning or cabins that feel smaller than the photographs suggested. A few new services were cancelled after a single season because too few tickets had been sold.

Yet the people who use these trains tend to become loyal. Many describe the experience as a small adventure: the sound of the wheels, the changing landscape at dawn, the conversations in the restaurant car with strangers they will never meet again. For them, the slowness is not a defect but the whole point. It turns a journey into a memory rather than a gap between two appointments.

Whether night trains will become a common choice once more depends on prices, on investment and on the patience of travellers. What seems certain is that the idea of sleeping your way across a continent has not lost its charm. Somewhere tonight, a train is leaving a quiet platform, and its passengers will wake up somewhere else.
